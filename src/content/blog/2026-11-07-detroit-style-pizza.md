I lived in the Detroit area from 2017 to 2019. I love Jet's and Buddy's, and I have wanted to make Detroit-style pizza at home ever since. I finally got a 10 × 14-inch Lloyd pan. Now I need dough worthy of the corners.

The part I want to get right is the crust: a light center with some chew, a crisp bottom, and cheese browned against the sides of the pan. Toppings can wait. The first project is flour, water, yeast, salt, and enough time.

**Working draft:** This is the formula I plan to test in the new pan. I have not baked this version yet. Jet's and Buddy's are the inspiration; this is my starting point for a home recipe.

## Does the dough take one or two days?

That is the plan for this version. The dough gets **24–48 hours in the fridge**, followed by about **3–4 hours to warm, stretch, and rise in the pan**. Most of that time needs no work. Allow about one hour for the first mixing and folding session, then 20–25 minutes to bake and 5 minutes to rest. Total elapsed time is roughly **29–54 hours**. The prep time above counts active work and excludes the long rests.

A same-day Detroit pizza is possible. [King Arthur's weeknight recipe](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/recipes/weeknight-detroit-pizza-recipe) is one example. I want to start with a cold rest so I can mix the dough one day and bake it the next. [Billy Zureikat's Detroit dough](https://www.trippingbillyz.com/recipes/detroit-style-dough) also uses a 24–48-hour fridge rest.

For Saturday dinner, mix on Friday afternoon and refrigerate by about 2 p.m. Take it out at 2 p.m. Saturday and aim to bake around 6 p.m. For the longer rest, mix on Thursday instead. A cool kitchen can add time on baking day; the dough's rise matters more than the clock.

## The dough formula

For this first batch, I am starting at **70% hydration**: 280 grams of water for 400 grams of flour. That means a soft, sticky dough. Do not add flour just to make it feel like a dry dough ball.

| Ingredient | Weight | Baker's percentage |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Bread flour | 400 g | 100% |
| Water | 280 g | 70% |
| Fine sea salt | 8 g | 2% |
| Instant yeast | 1.5 g | 0.375% |
| Olive oil, in the dough | 8 g | 2% |

Baker's percentages compare each ingredient with the flour weight. The mixed dough weighs about **698 grams**. Oil for the container and pan is separate.

Use bread flour for this test. A scale will make it easier to repeat the batch, especially for the flour. Cup and spoon amounts below are approximate; flour packing and salt grain size change the weight. The folds build strength without a mixer. The final rise in the pan gives the dough time to fill out before it carries the toppings.

## Ingredients

### Dough

- **400 g bread flour**, about **3⅓ cups**, spooned into the cup and leveled
- **280 g water**, about **1 cup plus 3 tablespoons**, at cool room temperature
- **8 g fine sea salt**, about **1⅓ teaspoons**; use the weight if substituting kosher salt
- **1.5 g instant yeast**, about **½ teaspoon**; use instant yeast for this schedule
- **8 g olive oil**, about **2 teaspoons**, for the dough

### Container and pan

- **1 teaspoon olive oil**, for the dough container
- **2 tablespoons olive oil**, for the 10 × 14-inch pan

### Toppings

- **12 ounces (340 g) young Wisconsin brick cheese**, about **3 cups shredded**; use mild, unaged brick cheese
- Or replace all the brick cheese with **8 ounces (225 g) low-moisture whole-milk mozzarella, about 2 cups shredded**, plus **4 ounces (115 g) mild white cheddar, about 1 cup shredded**
- **¾ cup thick prepared pizza sauce**, at room temperature
- **2 ounces (55 g) sliced pepperoni**, about **½ cup loosely packed**, optional

The cheese blend is a practical substitute. Avoid fresh mozzarella for this first test; it adds more water. Keep the toppings simple so it is easier to judge the dough.

## Instructions

1. **Mix the dough.** In a large bowl, stir together **400 g bread flour (about 3⅓ cups), 8 g fine sea salt (about 1⅓ teaspoons), and 1.5 g instant yeast (about ½ teaspoon)**. Add **280 g water (about 1 cup plus 3 tablespoons)** and **8 g olive oil (about 2 teaspoons)**. Mix with a sturdy spoon until no dry flour remains. Scrape down the bowl, cover, and rest for **20 minutes**. The dough will look rough and feel sticky.

2. **Fold three times.** Wet your fingers, lift one side of the dough, and fold it over the center. Turn the bowl and repeat on the other three sides. This is one set. Cover and rest for **20 minutes**, then do a second set. Rest another **20 minutes**, then do a third set. The dough should hold together better after each set. Stretch only as far as it allows without tearing; do not knead in extra flour.

3. **Refrigerate for 24–48 hours.** Coat a covered food container with **1 teaspoon olive oil**. Transfer the dough, turn it once to coat, and close the lid. Use a container with room for the dough to double. Refrigerate after the last fold. Some bubbles and expansion are expected, but it does not need to double in the fridge. For the first bake, aim for **24 hours** so there is a clear starting point for the next batch.

4. **Oil the pan and start the final rise.** About **4 hours before baking**, spread **2 tablespoons olive oil** across the bottom and lower sides of the **10 × 14-inch pan**. Transfer the cold dough to the pan. Turn it once in the oil, then gently press it into a broad rectangle. Cover without pressing the cover onto the dough and leave it for **45–60 minutes**. It probably will not reach the corners yet.

5. **Stretch, then let it rise.** Gently press the dough outward with your fingertips. If it pulls back, cover it and wait **20 minutes** before trying again. Once it reaches the corners, leave it covered until visibly puffy, with bubbles near the edges and a slight wobble when the pan moves. Allow about **3–4 hours total from fridge to oven**, including the rests in the previous step. In a cool room, allow longer. Do not flatten the dough again after this rise. If it is already very puffy earlier, move on to baking.

6. **Heat the oven.** During the last hour of the rise, put a rack in the lower third of the oven and heat to **500°F (260°C), conventional heat**. Allow at least **45 minutes** to preheat. Start with the pan directly on the rack for this test. Check the temperature limit supplied with your pan before use.

7. **Add the toppings.** If using pepperoni, distribute the full **2 ounces (55 g; about ½ cup)** over the dough. Add all **12 ounces (340 g; about 3 cups) shredded brick cheese**, carrying it right to the pan walls. If using the substitute, use all **8 ounces (225 g; about 2 cups) mozzarella and 4 ounces (115 g; about 1 cup) cheddar** instead. Spoon the **¾ cup pizza sauce** into three long stripes, using **¼ cup per stripe**. Keep your touch light so the dough holds its air.

8. **Bake until the bottom is crisp.** Bake at **500°F (260°C)** for about **20–25 minutes**, checking from **18 minutes**. Rotate once if your oven browns unevenly. Look for bubbling cheese, deep brown edges, and a browned, firm underside. Carefully lift a corner with a heat-safe spatula to check the bottom. If the top is ready but the base is pale, loosely cover the top with foil and give it a few more minutes on a lower rack. Record the actual time for this pan and oven.

9. **Remove from the pan and rest.** Let the pizza stand for **2 minutes**, then loosen the cheese around the sides with a spatula suitable for the pan's finish. Lift the pizza onto a wire rack and rest for another **3 minutes**. Transfer to a cutting board and cut into **8 squares**. The rack lets steam escape before it softens the bottom. Four corners, eight slices: plan accordingly.

## What to watch in the first batch

- **Dough keeps shrinking:** Give it another covered rest before stretching. Forcing it into the corners can tear it.
- **Dense center:** Check whether the dough was visibly puffy before topping. Note the room temperature and final rise time before changing the formula.
- **Gummy layer under the cheese:** Check the bake and sauce thickness. Extra toppings or a pale base can make the center harder to cook through.
- **Pale bottom with dark cheese:** Try a lower rack first on the next bake. A preheated baking steel is another variable to test later.
- **Dough rises and then collapses:** It may have gone too far. Record the fridge rest and room-temperature rise, then shorten the relevant stage next time.

For the first bake, I want notes on the flour brand, fridge time, final rise, oven rack, and bake time. Then I can compare a 24-hour dough with a 48-hour dough while keeping the rest the same. The useful question is whether the extra day improves the flavor and texture enough to repeat it.

## Pan and recipe notes

[Lloyd's standard Detroit pan](https://lloydpans.com/detroit-style-pizza-pans.html) is anodized aluminum with its PSTK finish. It does not need the seasoning routine used for bare blue steel. Follow the care instructions for the exact pan you bought.

[Kenji López-Alt's Detroit-style pizza guide](https://www.seriouseats.com/detroit-style-pizza-recipe) explains the value of bread flour, cheese at the pan walls, and strong bottom heat. [Shawn Randazzo's guide in Pizza Today](https://pizzatoday.com/news/detroit-style/130843/) describes cold fermentation as one route to Detroit-style dough. These are useful references for this test; the formula and schedule above still need a bake in my kitchen before I publish the recipe.
