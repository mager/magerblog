<div class="recipe-intro">

I had one of those restaurant salads that refuses to leave my head.

Longman & Eagle's greens look simple on paper, which is usually how you know the margin for error is small. Good greens, a light hand, and a dressing that lands exactly where it should. Sharp but not harsh. Rich but not heavy. Enough savoriness to make the whole bowl feel finished.

I went looking for more context and found that "WERP" is almost certainly **Werp Farm**, not the rooftop-project acronym I first heard in my head. Longman & Eagle has featured Werp Farm greens on different menus over time, sometimes with green goddess, sometimes in a greens salad with rosé vinaigrette. This version is not me pretending I have their exact house recipe. It is me trying to recreate the thing I actually wanted: a bright shallot vinaigrette that makes delicate greens taste much better than the ingredient list suggests.

</div>

<div class="recipe-body">
<div class="recipe-ingredients">

#### Dressing

- 1 small shallot, minced very finely
- 1/4 cup champagne vinegar or good white wine vinegar
- 1 tsp Dijon mustard
- 1 tsp honey
- 1/2 tsp kosher salt, plus more to taste
- 1/4 tsp freshly cracked black pepper
- 1/2 cup extra-virgin olive oil

#### For the salad

- Tender greens or microgreens
- A few soft herbs, torn
- Optional: thinly sliced radish and cucumber
- Flaky salt, if you want a little finishing texture

</div>

<div class="recipe-instructions">

Start with the shallot and vinegar in a small bowl or jar. Let that sit for 10 minutes. It is a small move, but it matters. The shallot softens, the vinegar picks up flavor, and the whole dressing gets more integrated.

Add the Dijon, honey, salt, and pepper. Whisk until the mustard loosens everything up.

Slowly stream in the olive oil while whisking until the dressing emulsifies. You want it glossy and cohesive, not split and oily.

Taste it on an actual leaf, not off a spoon. Salad dressing needs a target. It should read bright first, then round out on the back half. If it is too sharp, add a little more oil. If it feels dull, it usually wants salt before anything else.

Dress the greens lightly. This is important. The point is not to coat them like coleslaw. The point is to make them glisten.

A few notes from making this a couple times:

- Champagne vinegar gets closest to the clean, elegant version in my head.
- A tiny bit of honey helps, but this should not taste sweet.
- The shallot needs to be minced very fine or it takes over the texture.
- Good olive oil matters because there is nowhere for it to hide.

This is the kind of dressing that makes a bowl of greens feel expensive without doing anything complicated.

</div>

## What I think makes it work

The structure is classic vinaigrette, but the balance is the whole game.

Too much mustard and it starts reading deli-counter. Too much honey and it gets clumsy. Too much acid and the greens collapse before you finish eating. The version I like keeps all of those things on a short leash.

That is probably what I like so much about the Longman & Eagle version too. Restaurant greens like this are a useful reminder that "simple" usually means somebody made a lot of precise decisions.

## If you want to push it closer to restaurant-salad territory

A few easy variations:

- add a pinch of chopped chives or tarragon for more herb lift
- swap in lemon juice for a small part of the vinegar if you want it brighter and less wine-like
- use a mix of arugula, baby lettuce, and microgreens instead of one single box of spring mix
- finish with radish and cucumber if you want more crunch and temperature contrast

I would still keep the dressing restrained. The greens are the point.
