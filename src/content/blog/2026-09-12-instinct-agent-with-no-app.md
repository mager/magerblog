I've sent out a bunch of Instinct invites. If you want to try it, [use my invite link](https://app.instinct.com/invite?t=av6ems4arxgcqnbl5dxmwoeday). If that fills up, tell me. I'll ask Instinct for a fresh one.

[Instinct](https://instinct.com/) is a personal agent that runs in the cloud and that you can text or call. I use it through iMessage; it also works through WhatsApp. It connects to your accounts and devices and does work on your behalf. The part that caught my attention was how little I had to change my habits to use it.

My Mac mini is already my default, always-on box for agent workflows. I spend plenty of time in terminals, repositories, and browser sessions, configuring tools and giving agents access to things. I like having that control. But when I want help with something unrelated to building software, opening another workspace can feel like unnecessary preparation. With Instinct, I send a text.

The company describes an agent trained to operate a phone and computer, with access to connected applications and devices. Its examples include following up on conversations, arranging rides, and booking a handyman. It is still in private access, with a waitlist and invitations from existing members. Those are the product's claims; what interests me is how it fits into an ordinary conversation.

In iMessage, Instinct shows read receipts, reacts with emoji, and replies in threads. I am used to typing `/new` to start a fresh agent session, so I tried it here. It recognized the convention and responded, "Fresh slate it is. What's on your mind?" I didn't have to look up a different command.

I then asked it to search state unclaimed-property databases for money owed to me, using my name and the places I've lived. It acknowledged the request and said it would flag anything that needed my signature. This exchange shows the start of that task; I don't have a recovered-money result to report here.

I also had Instinct remove a spam botnet. That was an unglamorous cleanup task, and a useful reason to have an agent that could take action. I wanted the cleanup handled without turning it into another project on my list.

Separately, I asked it to help clean up my GitHub repositories. That request needed more judgment than simply deleting old code. If an old personal-site repository contained posts missing from my current magerblog repo, I wanted to keep it. I also wanted to preserve old startup projects and learning archives. Age alone wasn't a reason to throw something away.

Instinct acknowledged those constraints and began comparing the old sites with the current blog. The screenshot captures its response and part of its assessment, rather than a completed deletion report. What I like about the exchange is that I could explain what mattered in normal language and refine the task in the same thread.

Other people are trying similarly tedious jobs. [Messaging Agents collects public user reports](https://messagingagents.com/use-cases), including someone who said Instinct found unclaimed money and filed claims, and another who reported receiving airline miles after a delay. There are examples involving subscriptions, appointments, shopping, and inbox chores too. These are early users' accounts, not results I have independently checked, but I recognize the appeal: each job is manageable, yet easy to keep postponing.

It has also been useful when I just wanted to learn something. I saw a Hacker News post about icy moons and asked Instinct to read the article and the comments and summarize them. It gave me a summary, added a perspective of its own, and suggested more reading. I could follow that curiosity from the same conversation where I was asking for help with chores.

That is where I think the texting interface gets interesting. I think people will eventually text Instinct the way they text a friend: casually, asking for advice, asking for help, or following up on something from earlier. It's an agent, but as it earns your trust, I can see the conversation becoming less deliberate. You stop composing a task and just ask what's on your mind.

I'd like to see that extend to group chats. Friends could ask a question together, get an informed answer with something useful to read, and keep talking without leaving the conversation. I can imagine couples and families using it to work through a booking, compare options, or follow up on something nobody has had time to handle. Those are things I'd want from a shared agent; I haven't verified group-chat support in Instinct.

For that to work well, an agent would need to understand who is asking, what information belongs in the group, and whose approval an action requires. The convenience of a shared thread doesn't settle those questions. It does give the work a natural place to happen: where people are already coordinating.

Grokbot is another product I've been thinking about in this context. So is Meta's Muse. [WIRED's writeup](https://www.wired.com/story/meta-releases-muse-a-personal-ai-agent-with-privacy-built-into-it/) describes Muse as available through an app, the web, and WhatsApp, with the ability to send emails, book travel, and make purchases. It also describes Meta's approach to isolated virtual machines and human approval for actions. I had trouble signing in because my Facebook account is deactivated, so I haven't given Muse a proper try yet. I may try it next.

The trust questions matter even in a one-to-one conversation. Instinct's [privacy policy](https://instinct.com/privacy-policy) says it may access connected services, messages, emails, enabled audio, and location you choose to share. It also describes receiving credentials for third-party accounts. Information can be used to train its models unless you opt out; that opt-out applies going forward and has an exception for material flagged for safety review. Data received directly from Google Workspace is excluded from model training.

Its [terms](https://instinct.com/terms) authorize actions on connected services and warn that mistakes may not be reversible. They also distinguish disconnecting a service from requesting deletion of data already indexed. These are concrete settings and behaviors I want to understand before handing over more of my life.

[Messaging Agents' review of Instinct's disclosures](https://messagingagents.com/guides/instinct-what-it-publishes) points to unanswered questions around retention, deletion timelines, and security contacts. I also want clear answers in the product: what did it access, what did it do, what needs my approval, and what happens when a page or message contains instructions aimed at misleading the agent? A familiar messaging interface makes delegation easier; it doesn't answer those questions by itself.

I don't know whether Instinct will be the product I keep using a year from now. It is early, and there are several versions of this idea arriving at once. What I want to keep is the ease of the interaction: I can ask for help, add a constraint, follow up later, or change the subject without moving into a different workspace. If it keeps handling those exchanges reliably, I can see myself reaching for it more often, for smaller questions as well as chores.
